\begin{abstract}

\textbf{\color{red}NOTE: Red notes are important! Do not submit the document with any of them remaining!\color{black}}

The nature of Titan's surface is poorly understood, largely due to the atmosphere's extensive interference. To handle this interference, we simulate Lambertian Titan models using the radiative transfer program \srtcppspace at different surface albedos. We then compare these models to real data from Cassini VIMS (Visual and Infrared Mapping Spectrometer) of the Huygens landing site and equatorial dunes. We confirm that \srtcppspace produces reasonable simulations for Lambertian surfaces shrouded by Titan's atmosphere in many, but not all, situations. Furthermore, we find that Titan's equatorial dunes behave as Lambertian surfaces, with all identified deviations correlating to suspected atmospheric model deficiencies. We retrieve the surface albedos of Titan's dunes in the eight IR (infrared) atmospheric windows and find the albedos largely consistent with multiple prior works, but inconsistent with other prior works, revealing a disagreement over what the surface albedos of Titan's dunes truly are. Lastly, we perform a targeted search for an opposition effect on Titan's dunes through VIMS observations and find none, though the narrowness of the effect may make it unobservable.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Titan has one of the least understood surfaces in the entire Solar System, due largely to its thick haze-filled atmosphere that is opaque to most light. While there do exist a handful of atmospheric ``windows'' through which specific wavelengths of light can pass relatively unimpeded \citep{Barnes2007}, they permit us to glean only limited spectral information on the surface. Even within the windows, the thick atmosphere contaminates the relatively small amount of surface information we do receive. Transmission is unavoidably a combination of surface and atmospheric effects \citep{EsSayeh2023}. 

To separate surface and atmosphere contributions, we turn to radiative transfer models of Titan. These models calculate how Titan's atmosphere alters light passing through it, including any effects the surface might have as light interacts with it on the journey to a modeled detector. These models allow for true surface effect identification by comparing real data to the simulations. Many groups have created radiative transfer models over the years, each with their own strengths and weaknesses (\cite{Vinatier2007, Griffith2012, Xu2013, Barnes2018a, Rodriguez2018, Corlies2021, Rannou2021}; and \cite{EsSayeh2023}; among others). These radiative transfer models depend on accurate knowledge of Titan's atmosphere. We have the best measurements at the moon's equatorial regions where the Huygens lander sampled the atmosphere in situ \citep{Tomasko2008}.

Many groups have performed surface characterization studies attempting to filter out the influence of the atmosphere in the past \citep{McCord2006, Buratti2006, Soderblom2009, Kazeminejad2011, Bonnefoy2016, Brossier2018, EsSayeh2023, Solomonidou2024}. However, the majority of studies assume that the surface behaves as a Lambertian reflector, an isotropic scatterer with no directly reflected or backscattered components. \cite{Buratti2006} is a notable exception.

Few, if any, surfaces in the Solar System exhibit truly Lambertian behavior, largely due to the prevalent opposition effect \citep{Deau2009, Schroder2009}. We might expect Titan's surface to exhibit similar non-Lambertian behaviors, but first-order analysis of the moon as a whole in the IR (infrared) shows a Lambertian surface \citep{LeMoulic2019, LeMoulic2012}.

On the other hand, the one observation we have from the ground on Titan, the Huygens lander, did report an opposition effect \citep{Schroder2009, Karkoschka2012}. However, Huygens landed in a "dark blue" region \citep{Rodriguez2006}, an uncommon terrain on Titan's surface \citep{Keller2008}. For Titan as a whole, Radar observations show an opposition effect \citep{Neish2010, Wye2011}, but given the drastically different length scales probed between radar (centimeters) and IR (microns), we have no reason to expect similar reflectance behavior. Furthermore, other ways to deviate from Lambertian behavior exist, such as a forward-scattering component to the surface phase function, which may result from wetted terrains, for example \citep{Dhingra2020}.

Our primary science goal within this paper is to better understand Titan's surface properties by creating Lambertian Titan radiative transfer simulations and comparing them to real observations of the moon's equatorial dunes. We accomplish our goal through identifying the degree of Lambertian behavior from Titan's dunes in reflected IR sunlight. In the process of this Lambertian analysis, we gather information about the dunes' abledo in various IR wavelength windows. We also identify differing behaviors between said windows.

The highest-quality observations of Titan's surface come from the Cassini mission, observations which have a wide spread of viewing gometries. Having such a variety of observations helps our characterization of Titan's surface, making it easier to determine how non-Lambertian a terrain is. Most current radiative transfer models applicable to Titan either assume a plane parallel atmosphere in their calculations \citep{Griffith2012, Rodriguez2018, EsSayeh2023}, don't consider angle at all \citep{Vinatier2007, Rannou2021}, or use a spherical approximation \citep{Corlies2021}. Thus, all these radiative transfer models lose accuracy at high incidence and/or emission angles, and would miss potential non-Lambertian effects.

To gain the useful information contained within observations at oblique viewing geometries, we must consider the spherical nature of Titan's atmosphere. Thus, we create forward-modeled simulations of a Lambertian Titan using \srtcppspace (Spherical Radiative Transfer in C++), a radiative transfer code tailored to model Titan in full spherical geometry at the infrared wavelengths available to Cassini's VIMS (Visual and Infrared Mapping Spectrometer) instrument \citep{Barnes2018a}. Other spherical models exist \citep{Xu2013}, but we choose \srtcppspace due to familiarity with the code.

Now equipped with a spherical radiative transfer model and atmospheric characterization from Huygens, we can compare simulations with reality on a scale covering the entire Cassini mission. As the equatorial regions have the best characterized atmosphere, we choose to examine that terrain. While we examine multiple terrain types, we determine that the equatorial dunes have the highest number of reliable observations from which to draw meaningful conclusions.

We also hand-pick observations from the Huygens landing site for the sake of comparison, even though that location was only observed at a limited number of viewing geometries. For both the dunes and the Huygens landing site, we compile VIMS observations from across the entire Cassini mission over all available viewing geometries, limiting observations to only those we judged to have sufficient quality.

In addition to uncovering Titan's surface properties, this work serves a secondary purpose--to validate the \srtcppspace simulation against real data. We validate in two ways: qualitatively comparing the behavior of the dunes with the behavior of the simulations, and retrieving surface albedo to quantitatively check against previous groups' estimates.

In this paper, we first outline improvements made to \srtcppspace in Section \ref{sec:model} and report on the results of those changes in Section \ref{sec:mresults}. We describe our Titan data in Section \ref{sec:observe} and compare observation to simulation in Section \ref{sec:compare} for both the Huygens landing site and equatorial dunes, while also retrieving surface albedos for the dunes and checking them against prior work. Lastly, we conclude in Section \ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Radiative Transfer Model Methods} \label{sec:model}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fullspectrumcompare_CIA_eqbright}
\centering
\caption{Here we compare a \srtcppspace model Titan spectrum (red) to an observed spectrum (black) acquired by Cassini VIMS on the T81 flyby (2012 January 30) of Adiri, a bright highlands region near the Huygens and Dragonfly landing sites.  The incorporation of correlated-k coefficients to model atmospheric absorption follows \citet{EsSayeh2023}, including the model atmosphere and surface albedo inputs. We did not tune the surface albedos and atmospheric parameters to fit the observation spectrum; rather, the intent of this plot is more to show the strong general agreement between observations and the model instead of reproducing this particular spectrum.}
\label{fig:SRTCvalidate}
\end{figure}

Spherical Radiative Transfer in C++, or \srtcpp, is a fully spherical
Monte-Carlo radiative transfer model designed to address problems requiring
spatial variability in properties and/or high incidence and emission angles \citep{Barnes2018a}.  It computes in parallel the propagation, gaseous absorption, and aerosol scattering for billions of photon packets to build up synthetic images of Titan at designated wavelengths.  The strength of \srtcppspace lies in its ability to treat spatially variagated atmospheres in cases where extreme geometries affect output and to solve spatial problems moreso than spectral problems.  The \srtcppspace algorithm has previously simulated trans-terminator surface illumination on Titan \citep{Barnes2018b}, the effect of atmospheric scattering on surface imaging from Titan orbiters \citep{2020PSJ.....1...24B}, and explorations of Titan's adjacency effect \citep{2025PSJ.....6..194M}.  

We upgraded \srtcpp's capabilities by adding gaseous absorption, thereby allowing for it to model complete spectra.  We model the absorption using the correlated-k approach outlined by \citet{EsSayeh2023} by an explicit numerical integral of extinctions in four Gauss orders along the flight path of each photon packet.  The resulting opacity adds to that calculated from haze scattering on each segment traversed.

We show an example of \srtcppspace results in this new spectral mode in Figure \ref{fig:SRTCvalidate}.  Figure \ref{fig:SRTCvalidate} compares a generic forward-modeled Titan spectrum from \srtcppspace using standard \citet{EsSayeh2023} inputs to a typical observed Titan spectrum from Cassini VIMS on the T81 flyby.  In general the two spectra show strong agreement, although with some discrepancies at the longward end of atmospheric windows (``known unknown" absorbers, \citet{2026PSJ.....7...54B}) and in the shortest wavelength windows where the effects of Rayleigh scattering (as of yet unmodeled by \srtcpp) come into play. 

For our work in this paper, we use \srtcppspace to create three different simulations of a uniformly Lambertian Titan, each run with a different surface albedo: 0.0, 0.1, and 0.2. Besides the albedo, the simulations have identical parameters, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:SRTClayout}. The virtual detectors sit 10,000 km away from the Lambertian Titan, separated by 5 degrees and completely encircle the moon. The simulation outputs at all eight VIMS IR wavelength windows for Titan's atmosphere: 0.93, 1.08, 1.27, 1.59, 2.01, 2.69, 2.79, and 5.00 $\mathrm{\mu}$m.

The simulations generally follow expectation for a Lambertian sphere obscured by a thick atmosphere. \cite{Pont2007} show what a Lambertian sphere should look like without an atmosphere in their Figure 9, and our 0.0 albedo simulation shows us the effect of the atmosphere without any signal coming from the surface. We expect both atmospheric and surface effects in the 0.1 albedo simulation, which we do see in the zero phase view: a flat greenish center with minimal atmospheric influence that slowly fades off as the emission angle becomes steeper, at which point the atmosphere takes over with its bluish hue.

Haze scattering dominates the overall coloration; atmospheric haze scatters bluer light more readily than redder light. In the raw data, the blue channel exhibits the highest brightness even in the center at 0.1 albedo. The chosen color balancing gives Titan its greenish hue, a sensible choice as it allows us to readily identify places with more surface signal than atmospheric. The center of the disc, when viewed at zero phase, is generally uniform with small gradients toward the limb. 

Views other than at zero phase also behave regularly; atmospheric effects dominate the linb, making it bluer. Light forward scatters off of Titan's haze particles more often than it backscatters \citep{GarcaMuoz2017, Cooper2025}, so that the closer the Sun gets to behind Titan, the brighter the limb becomes. A Lambertian surface without an atmosphere would have no light coming from anywhere that was not directly illuminated, but we see a surprisingly large amount of light from beyond the terminator due to atmospheric scattering, an effect also seen in real observations.

We can also see the shadow Titan casts on its own atmosphere in the profile views by examining the abruptly diminished signal near the top and bottom of the moon, while the atmosphere above this shadow remains brightly illuminated. This effect happens on Earth as well; it causes the colors of twilight \citep{Lynch2004}.

The simulation results appear slightly grainy due to the Monte-Carlo nature of \srtcppspace. If we were to run the simulation for longer, the S/N (signal-to-noise ratio) would increase, and the overall image result would become smoother, albeit at the cost of comptuer time. The simulations that we use represent a compromise between S/N and CPU-months.

We ultimately seek to compare these simulations with real data. While we can qualitatively see that real Titan images have similar coloration and limb effects \citep{Barnes2005}, the fact that the real Titan has numerous different terrain types interferes with any more robust conclusions. Thus, we transform the simulations to present their data based on viewing geometry, rather than direct visual images. The transformed simulations can, given a specific viewing geometry, describe how a Lambertian Titan would appear at that geometry. The simulations effectively transform into numerical bidirectional reflectance distribution functions (BRDFs).

To construct these transformed simulations, we sort every spectel into bins based on viewing geometry, and then we average their values. We separate the bins by 5$^{\circ}$ increments in incidence, emission, and azimuth angle, defined as shown in Figure \ref{fig:displaylabel}, with 0$^{\circ}$ azimuth being the forward-scattering direction, and 180$^{\circ}$ being backscattering. This process increases the S/N for the amalgamated spectels, ameliorating the shot noise in the simulations.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{VGDisplayLabel.pdf}

\centering

\caption{Viewing geometry angles used in this paper. The incidence angle is represented by $i$, the emission angle by $e$, and the azimuth angle by $\phi$. The arrows trace out a path from the sun (yellow circle) to some arbitrary spot on Titan's surface before and then to a detector (purple diamond). Diagrams modeled after this one appear in subsequent figures to give context for the data.}

\label{fig:displaylabel}

\end{figure}

Notably, our transformed simulations use three angles, while more traditional BRDFs have four. We do not treat topographic changes in a surface due to the information's heavy dependence on context, so we can safely assume that the Sunlight always comes in from the same relative direction, removing the need to keep track of the fourth viewing angle. As such, we will call these transformed simulations restricted BRDFs from here on out. This choice also makes it easier to determine when backscattering or forward-scattering occurs, key indicators of non-Lambertian behavior.

We also differ from traditional BRDFs in another way: we retain the atmospheric contributions. We produce the apparent response Titan has to light when observed from outside the atmosphere, not the direct response of the surface.

With 3 different albedos and 8 wavelengths, we have a total of 24 restricted BRDFs ready for comparison with other restricted BRDFs compiled from real VIMS data.

\section{Observations} \label{sec:observe}

Cassini performed 127 close flybys of Titan during its mission \citep{Seal2017}, and most of those flybys have observations from Cassini VIMS. Orbital mechanics limits the viewing geometries available on any single flyby. Thus, in order to gain a proper understanding of the surface of Titan at a wide range of viewing geometries, we use observations from as many flybys as possible.

We investigate the equatorial regions between 30$^{\circ}$N and 30$^{\circ}$S latitude, as this includes the Huygens landing site where Huygens' directly sampled the atmosphere, making it the best characterized region on Titan \citep{Tomasko2008}. We also want terrain types that Cassini examined from a wide variety of viewing geometries with a large number of reliable observations. We judge that Titan's equatorial dunes best meet all these criteria. To gather the relevant data, we create a raster mask of Titan's equatorial dunes in Figure \ref{fig:dunemask} at a resolution of one pixel per degree on Titan's surface; 61 pixels high in latitude and 360 wide in longitude.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TitanSurfaceMaskDunes.pdf}

\centering

\caption{Titan equatorial surface terrain mask for dunes, as informed by \cite{Lopes2020} mixed with VIMS observations. The red pixels are dunes; black pixels are everything else. Almost all of the dunes on Titan exist in this mask, though a few small regions lie outside the 30$^{\circ}$ and -30$^{\circ}$ latitude boundaries.}

\label{fig:dunemask}

\end{figure*}

The creation of the mask begins with the Titan terrain map created by \cite{Lopes2020} using radar data. VIMS observations, taken in IR, often don't match the radar observations \citep{Soderblom2007}, but tend to agree in the bulk of major features. Most notably (and importantly for this paper), the dunes have good agreement on a global scale \citep{Soderblom2007, Radebaugh2013, Rodriguez2014}.

Our investigation procedure for the VIMS observations begins with a basic database search; in our specific case, we look for any images that have pixels in the equatorial regions between 30$^{\circ}$N and 30$^{\circ}$S latitude, where those pixels have 25 km ground resolution or finer. The database we use has already filtered out clearly erroneous olbservations, as well as any images that only had a handful of pixels in height. We calibrate the images with the standard VIMS pipeline \citep{Barnes2007}.

We put each pixel that matches our criteria into a list. We then bin spectral components of each listed pixel and create a restricted BRDF out of them in a process identical to that described in Section \ref{sec:mresults}. Although we ran the simulations at precisely 5.00 $\mu$m, for VIMS observations we average together sixteen separate channels, taking advantage of the large width of the 5 $\mu$m window to increase the S/N. Unlike many other investigations \citep{Buratti2006, Bonnefoy2016, Brossier2018, EsSayeh2023}, we do not attempt to subtract off or correct for atmospheric contributions, as our simulations include those effects.

The restricted BRDFs have a few limitations. Primarily, bins at certain viewing angles, usually at the extreme ends of allowed values, have no data since Cassini was never in those positions. Also, no checks for interfering clouds occur while amalgamating surface observations.

Coarse observations can mix spectral end members together while particularly fine-resolution images can separate them; for instance, the handful of VIMS observations that can see interdune areas \citep{Barnes2008}. Pure spectral endmembers need not match the behavior of the surrounding terrain, and could conceivably offset the final model. Fortunately, even though the interdune areas vary considerably across Titan \citep{Bonnefoy2016}, the restricted BRDFs created for the dunes exhibit remarkable consistency and order.

We expect that any retrieved albedos from this model will most likely exhibit bias higher than pure dune sands, given the brighter interdunes \citep{Bonnefoy2016}. However, as we examine the dunes terrain type as a whole, we do not consider this bias a detriment to any of our results.

In addition to the dunes, we also derive restricted BRDFs for the Huygens landing site, allowing for more direct comparisons to observations made by the Huygens lander \citep{Tomasko2008}. We perform a database search similarly to how we make the dunes models, but instead of using the mask, we manually clean up any situations where we obtain the wrong latitude and longitude due to ephemeris uncertainties. This manual cleaning ensures the removal of contamination in the data; a caution we only exercise because of the Huygens landing site's small area on the boundary of multiple terrain types.

To counteract the lack of observational geometries for both the equatorial dunes and the Huygens landing site, we tetrahedrally interpolate to fill in as many observation angles as possible in the restricted BRDFs using the PyVista package \citep{Sullivan2019}.

\section{Simulation vs Data Comparison} \label{sec:compare}

The simulations' geometries naturally extend far beyond those actually aquired by Cassini. All three skewer types show a distinct shifting of behavior as a function of wavelength. In general, shorter wavelengths show higher I/F as light traverses through a higher optical depth of highly reflective haze aerosols \citep{EsSayeh2023}, allowing for more scattering events in more directions. Longer wavelengths usually exhibit lower I/F, though 5.00 $\mu$m is often slightly brighter than 2.79 $\mu$m.

Incidence skewers almost always have a characteristic pattern, with the three different albedo simulations spread out in I/F at low incidence, lowering in I/F and coming closer together as incidence increases, until at the highest incidences the simulations become indistinguishable from each other. Figure \ref{fig:inciskewer}'s skewers with emission fixed at 35$^{\circ}$ and with azimuth fized at 55$^{\circ}$ exemplify the general incidence behavior.

Emission skewers follow a similar but inverted pattern to the incidence skewers and see more variability between the different windows. As a general rule, lower emission angles show the different albedo simulations sperad out with relatively low I/F, and high emission has the simulations closer together with relatively high I/F. The shortest wavelengths exhibit minimal difference between the simulations' I/F, but I/F still rises with emission. The longer wavelengths start to deviate from the pattern where the 0.2 albedo simulation trends downward in I/F with higher emission, while the 0.1 albedo simulation lies flat, and only the 0.0 albedo simulation rises. 5.00 $\mu$m almost always behaves this way. We attribute this behavior to atmospheric haze undergoing limb brightening at high emission, while at the same time surface features become limb darkened. Surface features have a greater influence on the clearer, longer-wavelength windows, hence the change in simulation behavior. The other long wavelengths change behavior based on the specific viewing geometry. Figure \ref{fig:emisskewer}'s emission skewers with emission fixed at 35$^{\circ}$ and azimuth fixed at 50$^{\circ}$ exemplify this curious long wavelength behavior.

Azimuth skewers tend to be mostly flat in the simulation results, a behavior exemplified by Figure \ref{fig:azimskewer}'s skewers at 35$^{\circ}$ incidence and 30$^{\circ}$ emission. Azimuth skewer behavior changes when both incidence and emission approach larger values, as the forward-scattering haze begins to take effect.

Across these three figures, we display only a small selection of the total three-dimensional histogram; behavior can change significantly when skewering at different incidence, emission, or azimuth angles, though the transitions are always relatively smooth. We chose these particular skewers because they have the best Huygens landing site data.

The Huygens landing site data behaves well, for the most part, interpolating smoothly and forming lines that correspond rather well to the simulations. In 1.27 and 2.01 $\mu$m, the data line up almost perfectly with the 0.1 albedo simulation. The 1.59, 2.69, 2.79 and 5 $\mu$m windows have around 0.03 albedo.

The 1.08 and 0.93 $\mu$m data hug the 0.0 albedo line from the simulations, and of all the wavelengths, they show the largest deviations from parallel to the simulation, most notable in Figure \ref{fig:emisskewer}'s emission skewers with incidence fixed at 35$^{\circ}$ and azimuth fixed at 50$^{\circ}$, where the observational data dips below 0.0 albedo. We expected deviations for these two shortest of windows due to large atmospheric contributions, excess noise, and unmodeled Rayleigh scattering.

Even considering the deviations at short wavelengths, the Huygens landing site appears consistent with a Lambertian surface in the limited viewing geometries to which we have access. However, considering the relatively small section of the range these viewing gometries cover, we are skeptical of these results. We chose the best skewers we could for Figures \ref{fig:inciskewer}-\ref{fig:azimskewer}, and even the azimuth skewers cover less than half of the available values. Cassini's observations of the Huygens landing site don't probe the higher incidence and emission angles, and we don't even approach the extremes. Thus, we move to a much larger data set: the equatorial dunes.

\subsection{Equatorial Dunes} \label{sec:dunesC}

To retrieve the surface albedo, we make use of the three different albedos of the simulations: 0.0, 0.1, and 0.2. When the equatorial dunes data point lies between these points, we linearly interpolate to obtain an albedo value for the dunes' surface, and if the real data point is below the 0.0 albedo simulation or above the 0.2 albedo simulation, we extrapolate using a linear fit to retrieve the albedo. The fit sometimes creates unphysical negative retrieved albedos. We keep these negative values, so when plotted, some indication remains of how far below the predicted 0.0 albedo simulation the real dunes lie.

Examining the results, we find that the dunes data match the shape of the simulations rather closely in the incidence skewer views, as we can see in the skewers of Figure \ref{fig:dunesinciskewer} with fixed 5$^{\circ}$ emission and fixed 50$^{\circ}$ azimuth. Incidence angles higher than 80$^{\circ}$ should be taken with a grain of salt, as Cassini acquired few observations near and beyond the terminator. When we examine the full set of data, we see some minor deviations in incidence skewers at high emission and high azimuth, but the limited observations in those geometries make it difficult to draw conclusions from the discrepancy, if the discrepancy truly exists.

The incidence skewers also clearly show a physical impossibility that sometimes crops up: multiple observations for 0.93 and 1.08 $\mu$m dip below the 0.0 albedo simulation, resulting in a negative retrieved albedo. This retrieval occurs somewhat regularly within the shorter wavelength windows. In Figure \ref{fig:Dome}, the slice taken with incidence fixed at 55$^{\circ}$, shows quite a few negative retrieved albedos in the 1.27 $\mu$m window. Considering that we predicted that the interdunes would brighten what we saw in the dunes observations, these overly dim retrievals come as a surprise.

Now, in some cases, the negative retrieved albedo occurs because the surface ceases to have a significant result on the simulations: at 100$^{\circ}$ incidence, the simulations do not show variation with albedo due to their atmospherically-dominated nature, forcing an unrealistic extrapolation. Such extrapolations happen at high emission angles as well, though to a lesser extent. Unfortunately, the unrealistic extrapolations do not explain every negative albedo, as the slice with fixed 55$^{\circ}$ incidence at 1.27 $\mu$m in Figure \ref{fig:Dome} shows many negative albedos, and we have clear examples of observations falling below the dimmest simulation in many skewers.

The lack of Rayleigh scattering in \srtcppspace  may explain the physically impossible abledo retrievals. Rayleigh scattering is most relevant at short wavelengths \citep{EsSayeh2023}, which commonly produce negative retrieved albedos. Intuitively, one would expect adding Rayleigh scattering to make the simulation brighter, not dimmer, but subtle effects could be at play--to fully answer this question, we would have to implement Rayleigh scattering, which is on the list for future improvements to \srtcppspace.

Notably, in the 1.27 $\mu$m, fixed 55$^{\circ}$ incidence angle slice of Figure \ref{fig:Dome}, the negative retrieved albedos cluster largely around the edge of the plot. Indeed, the plot as a whole shows a lowering of retrieved albedo with higher emission angle. We found this behavior in the data for the 0.93, 1.08, and 1.59 $\mu$m windows as well. This lowering of retrieved albedo with emission would not occur if the simulations accurately represented actual Titan. For a truly Lambertian surface, our procedure should retrieve relatively constant albedos, barring extreme situations where the simulations become nearly indistinguishable. Such a ideal cases appear in the 5 $\mu$m albedo retrievals, including our selection at fixed 35$^{\circ}$ incidence in Figure \ref{fig:Dome}. The 5 $\mu$m window produces largely consistent albedo retrievals; the only one of the eight windows to do so, potentially because of its lack of atmospheric influence.

If we examine the other remaining windows (2.01, 2.69, and 2.79 $\mu$m), we find results akin to those in the slice at 2.01 $\mu$m, fixed 50$^{\circ}$ incidence in Figure \ref{fig:Dome}, where the retrieved albedo increases at higher emission angles. Emission skewers such as the fixed 75$^{\circ}$ incidence and fixed 175$^{\circ}$ azimuth set in Figure \ref{fig:dunesemisskewer} showcase this remarkably dramatic increasing of retrieved albedo with emission angle.

Up to around 40$^{\circ}$ emission, the dunes data match the simulations decently well, though the fixed 75$^{\circ}$ incidence and fixed 175$^{\circ}$ azimuth skewers in Figure \ref{fig:dunesemisskewer} continue to demonstrate I/F values below the 0.0 surface albedo simulation at short wavelengths.

At emission angles past 40$^{\circ}$, the simulations no longer broadly follow the observational data. The 0.93 - 1.59 $\mu$m windows dip further below the 0.0 albedo simulation as emission increases, while 2.01 - 2.79 $\mu$m do the opposite and sharply tick above the simulations. As we can see in Figure \ref{fig:Dome} at 1.27$\mu$m, fixed 55$^{\circ}$ incidence and at 2.01 $\mu$m, fixed 50$^{\circ}$ incidence, this effect is largely azimuthally independent, though the exact degree of the anomaly changes at forward or backscattering directions.

So, how can we explain the variability in retrieved abledo across the various emission angles? We identify three primary explanations: biased or contaminated data, real non-Lambertian surface properties, or inaccuracies in the atmospheric model.

We rule out iased or contaminated data rather quickly: the rise in retrieved surface albedo with emission exists in a wide variety of observations and viewing geometries, occurring at most azimuths. To check further, we divide up the restricted BRDFs based on the time of the observation, finding that the retrieved albedo still deviates with emission across the entire Cassini mission. Separating the data by latitude keeps the effect, as does a longitudinal separation via specific dune fields. We could not find any variation in time or location on Titan. Thus, we rule out biased or contaminated data.

The second explanation for the rising retrieved albedo, a true non-Lambertian effect, seems unlikely.  The brightening effect appears azimuthally agnostic: no matter what azimuth we consider, the emission effect exists where we have enough data to see it, and a forward-scattering or backscattering effect would focus at azimuths of 0$^{\circ}$ or 180$^{\circ}$, respectively, not manifest everywhere. Topography-oriented explanations also seem unlikely, since the effect begins around 40$^{\circ}$ and only extreme emission angles should see influence by the muted topography that Cassini found \citep{Corlies2017}.

That said, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility of true non-Lambertian effects, for the dunes could behave in a completely unexpected manner. If we can rule out all other options, we would consider an unexpected surface property explanation in future work by testing out various non-Lambertian BRDFs for the surface in the \srtcppspace simulations and observing if any of them led to azimuthally agnostic effects.

Lastly, we consider that the model may not accurately account for some atmospheric effect at high emission. While we mentioned Rayleigh scattering earlier, that will only have an important effect on short wavelengths \citep{EsSayeh2023}. We identify three potential places in the atmospheric model where the deficiency could lie: the haze density, the atmospheric single scattering albedo (SSA), or the scattering phase function itself.

We examine the haze density and single scattering albedo secnarios by running simulations that modify those parameters. Figure \ref{fig:hazeSSAtests} shows the results of altering the haze to 0.5 and 2.0 density, while adjusting the SSA by factors of 0.8 and 1.2. Since we only examine 2 $\mu$m here, which has SSA values of around 0.7, the factor of 1.2 cannot create unphysical SSA values larger than 1. Unfortunately, none of the adjusted models come close to fixing the issue. The 0.5 haze simulation adds a strange hump at low emission, 2.0 haze exhibits minimal change, and 0.8 SSA flattens out the curve. The 1.2 SSA simulation actually shows a potentially correct slope at higher emission, but at lower emission, it fails completely!

These unsuccessful simulations lead us to the conclusion that the likely source of the discrepancy likely lies in the atmospheric scattering phase function. Unlike the other possibilities, simply increasing and decreasing some parameter would not sufficiently test the phase function, for it has a wide variety of shapes that it could take, and any adjustments to it would require normalization. Exploration of the influences of altering the phase function is beyond the scope of this paper, so we leave that investigation to future work.

Any of the three considered parameters (haze density, SSA, and phase function) could vary altitudinally in a way inaccurately represented in the model, which could also cause the discrepancy. A combination effect from multiple parameters may also be involved. Investigating these possibilities is also left to future work.

Despite the clear deviation at high emission, overall, the simulation and dunes data still match remarkably well. The shape of the dunes data matches the incidence and azimuth skewer predictions, even if sometimes it sits below the 0.0 albedo simulation. Azimuth skewers in particular behave well, most exhibiting clearly flat behavior in the dunes data, such as in the skewers with incidence fixed at 40$^{\circ}$ and emission fixed at 30$^{\circ}$ in Figure \ref{fig:dunesazimskewer}.

With the shapes of the dunes data matching the restricted BRDFs so closely in incidence and azimuth, it seems reasonable to conclude that the dunes act substantially as a Lambertian surface. The primary evidence for non-Lambertian behavior comes from the inconsistent albedo retrieval at variable emission, which does not appear to have an azimuthal dependence, making it likely an atmospheric effect not accounted for in the simulation, rather than a true non-Lambertian effect.

Our albedo retrieval for 5 $\mu$m demonstrates consistency with all previous work. The double windows 2.69 and 2.79 $\mu$m show good agreement as well, though already at this point we can see prior work diverging into two separate camps: \cite{McCord2006}, \cite{EsSayeh2023}, and our work on one side; \cite{Bonnefoy2016}, \cite{Lopes2016}, and \cite{Brossier2018} on the other. \cite{Solomonidou2018} reports lower albedo values broadly consistent with ours when they consider multiple locaitons on Titan, but they also report values for a single location on Titan more consistent with the brighter albedo values of \cite{Bonnefoy2016}, \cite{Lopes2016}, and \cite{Brossier2018}.

We are unsure why we see such drastic differences between these retrievals. The different results in \cite{Solomonidou2018} suggest that only looking at a single place on Titan may bias the data. This interpretation is complicated by \cite{Bonnefoy2016}, which averages several different locations, retrieving albedos consistent with the brighter results, not the dimmer ones, which are where our result and \cite{Solomonidou2018}'s multiple locaitons result reside. Notably, \cite{Bonnefoy2016} and \cite{Solomonidou2018} cover far less area than our survey. While unlikely, the possibility exists that one or both surveys sampled a biased subset of Titan's dunes that had different albedo responses. We also have to consider bias in our data: while our results are global in nature, the restrictive nature of our mask ensures we never sample dunes anywhere near another terrain type, which can create bias if central dunes differ from the edges.

Model differences could easily cause such incongruities in retrieved albedo. However, \cite{Lopes2016, Solomonidou2018, Brossier2018} and \cite{EsSayeh2023} all originate from the same \cite{Hirtzig2013} model, yet these four produce both high-albedo and low-albedo retrievals. \cite{Lopes2016} and \cite{Solomonidou2018} appear to use near identical procedures to each other, and while some agreement exists between \cite{Solomonidou2018}'s single location results and \cite{Lopes2016}'s results, the agreement is inconsistent.

Leaving aside the brighter albedos for a moment, our results show broad agreement with the dimmer retrieved albedo values; even our retrievals for the 0.93 and 1.08 $\mu$m windows, which we identify as having poor coorelation with reality considering that their distributions dip significantly into negative retrieved albedos. Given the large error bars placed on measurements in this short wavelength regime, when retrieved albedos are even reported, we suggest that research should not use the 0.93 and 1.08 $\mu$m windows to draw conclusions about Titan's surface in isolation.

Upon noting the relative agreement our retrieved albedos have with three different sources, our simulation results appear reasonable. Though, noting the wide spread of results across multiple studies, we conclude that the community's knowledge of Titan's surface albedo is limited and likely to change as new techniques arise.

\subsection{Opposition Effect} \label{sec:oppC}

We recognize that our method of binning spectels together and averaging them all makes us significantly less sensitive to dramatic changes over tiny angular extents, such as the sharp central peak often seen in the opposition effect, sometimes less than one degree away from opposition \citep{Kulyk2008, Schaefer2008}. 

We perform a direct search across our entire database, image by image, for an opposition effect in the dunes (or an equivalent effect in the forward-scattering direction). We find no evidence for any such effect. We expect this for seven of the eight windows as diffuse lighting via scattering all but removes the opposition effect \citep{Schroder2008}. However, the eighth window, 5 $\mu$m, sees significant unimpeded sunlight even at sunset \citep{Barnes2018b}. Even here, we find no evidence of an opposition effect.

To be fair, no observation we consider gets closer to opposition than between 1 and 2 degrees. Thus, our observations do not rule out a narrow and sharp opposition effect, as confinement to within 0.1$^{\circ}$ can occur. \citep{Kulyk2008, Schaefer2008}. The solar disc's footprint on Titan sits around 0.05 $\deg$ \citep{Barnes2011}, further implying undetectability of the opposition effect. Huygens observed the opposition effect spike to begin around 0.2$^{\circ}$ \citep{Karkoschka2012}, and that observation was made with Huygens' own illumination, not the Sun's. Furthermore, Huygens did not land on the dunes. 

The viewing geometry to definitively confirm or deny the opposition effect does not exist in the Cassini VIMS data for the dunes; we can only say that if the dunes have an opposition effect, it is a narrow one with little-to-no broad component.

We also find no pseudo-specular forward-scattering component in the dunes, even though we did find an observation that approached zero degrees from the specular direction.

\section{Summary and Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

We have two primary purposes to this paper: to constrain Titan's surface dune properties and to validate the \srtcppspace simulations against real data.

On the validation front, \srtcppspace in general calculates restricted BRDF trends that match the real data in incidence and azimuth angles, but sometimes produces inconsistent albedos, as evidenced by situations where retrieve impossible albedos below 0.0. As these moments primarily occur at short wavelengths, the lack of modeled Rayleigh scattering could produce these impossible albedos. Alternatively, or perhaps additionally, the negative albedos could arise from the faulty characterization of atmospheric haze--the far more prevalent observed deviations with increasing emission angle imply as much.

More quantitatively, our general retrieved albedos for the dunes in each of the individual windows are largely consistent with previous work. Our inquiry as to why two different trends in retrieved surface albedos for the dunes exist across the literature was inconclusive. Our results corroborate the lower albedo values rather than the higher albedo values.

Despite these caveats, \srtcppspace still produces smooth lines parallel to the real data in most incidence and azimuth skewers, only with inconsistent surface albedo retrieval. As such, the simulations remain useful for characterizing Titan's surface, particularly when looking for clear deviations from the Lambertian assumption. We found almost no such deviations. We also found no evidence of an opposition effect and no evidence for a forward scattering component to the surface BRDF. The only major deviation we found is the dimming and brightening some windows exhibit at high emission angles--which is likely a haze model insufficiency rather than a real effect. The Huygens landing site also shows Lambertian behavior, though we do not say this with confidence considering the limited nature of the observations at that location.

We ultimately conclude that Titan's dunes are Lambertian surfaces, or very close to it. We expected this result, given the generally Lambertian nature of Earth's sand \citep{Hapke1963}, though it exhibits an opposition effect within two degrees of zero phase angle \citep{Wise2022}, which we would have seen if such an effect present on Titan. However, the width of an opposition effect likely narrows with distance from the Sun \citep{Karkoschka2012} due to the smaller angular diameter of the solar disk, so we cannot say that we should have seen an opposition effect on the dunes. Furthermore, characteristic differences exist between the dunes of Earth and Titan. Earth's deserts tend toward high albedos, while Titan's dunes exhibit the lowest albedo classification on Titan's solid surface.

Future work would include improving \srtcppspace's model of Titan's haze to simulate the atmosphere properly at high emission--or, if that were to prove impossible, investigating what other effects might cause the inaccuracies.

This work could also assist in alalysis of the specular lakes at Titan's north pole; while the atmosphere is not as well characterized there, we can use the straightforward specular lake surface phase functions to probe where exactly the most poorly characterized parameters are. We can also examine other terrain types on Titan--though the investigation would require a more manual rather than automated approach, as the other terrains often blend into each other without sharp distinctions.
